# Companies Around Me (4): Costco

Saturday morning. Costco parking lot. Already full at ten.

I circled twice before someone in a Subaru backed out. Grabbed a cart from the corral, one wheel wobbling. Inside, past the membership card check, past the seventy-inch TVs nobody came here for, straight to the back where the Kirkland mixed nuts live. Three-pound jar, $12.99. Was $11.49 last year. You don't stop buying it over a buck fifty. But you notice.

The sample lady was handing out little cups of some kind of mango smoothie. A kid took three. His mom pretended not to see. Two carts ahead of me, a guy had a flat of water, a case of toilet paper, and a rotisserie chicken balanced on top like a crown. $4.99 for that chicken. Has been $4.99 for years.

Costco loses money on every one of those chickens. They know. They don't care.

That's when I started wondering what actually pays for all of this.

---

Here's the number that stopped our team cold.

Costco sells over $250 billion worth of stuff a year. The net margin on all that merchandise is under 3%. A hundred dollars of groceries, and Costco keeps less than three.

So where does the money come from?

Your membership card. That $65 you pay every January and forget about. 147 million members worldwide, nearly $5 billion a year in fees. The margin on that revenue is close to 100%—no inventory, no shipping, no spoilage. Roughly three-quarters of Costco's net profit comes from that single line item.

The nuts, the chicken, the gas, the tires—all of it is bait. The membership fee is the catch.

A friend of mine runs a SaaS startup. I showed him the numbers over lunch. He put his fork down. "Ninety-two percent renewal rate, near-zero acquisition cost, lifetime value north of $1,700 per member. This isn't retail. This is the best subscription business I've ever seen."

He's not wrong. Except the product isn't software. It's the feeling that you can throw anything from Costco into your cart and it won't be a mistake.

Speaking of throwing things into the cart.

I did a rough inventory of my house last month. About half the consumables are Kirkland. Laundry detergent. Olive oil. Trash bags. Batteries. Coffee beans. My wife bought Kirkland diapers once, said they were the same as Pampers but a third cheaper. Word got around the mom group chat. The shelf was empty the next weekend.

Kirkland looks like a store brand. Most people treat it like one. But its annual sales are $90 billion.

To put that in context: Kraft Heinz—the company behind Heinz ketchup, Oscar Mayer, Maxwell House—does $26 billion a year. Kirkland is 3.4 times larger. If it were spun off tomorrow, it would be the sixth-largest consumer goods company in America.

You won't find it on any brand ranking. It only exists inside Costco. That's the whole point. Kirkland doesn't compete with brands on store shelves across the country. It competes with the brands on the same shelf, in a store that 147 million members already walk into every month.

There's a rumor that's been floating around forever: Kirkland vodka is actually made in the same distillery as Grey Goose. I've never been able to fully confirm it. But I can confirm this—Kirkland batteries come off a Duracell line. Same factory. Same spec. Different label. Forty percent cheaper.

You think you're buying generic. You're buying a hidden empire with a moat.

---

Now here's the part that changed how I think about this company.

Costco pays an average of $31.90 an hour. Federal minimum wage: $7.25. Walmart: roughly $17–18. Sam's Club: around $19.

First reaction: good for them. Nice company, treats people well.

It's not charity. It's math.

Costco's employee turnover is under 10%. The rest of the retail industry runs 60% to 80%. Walmart rehires and retrains more than half its workforce every single year. Costco barely has to.

Result: each Costco employee generates $1.07 million in revenue. Walmart: $290,000. Nearly four times less.

Selling, general, and administrative costs? Nine percent of revenue. Lowest in the industry. Walmart runs at twenty.

Pay more. Lose fewer people. Train less. Get more output per head. Spend less on overhead. The math is elegant and nearly impossible to copy—because Walmart's gross margin structure can't support a $32 hourly wage. If they tried, they'd bleed before they caught up.

It's a rare kind of advantage: **the more a competitor tries to imitate it, the worse off they get.**

---

Most American investors don't think much about Sam's Club. It's the Walmart-owned warehouse club. Decent. Not as cool. Smaller.

But something interesting is happening on the other side of the world.

I go back to China once or twice a year. Every trip, someone drags me to a Sam's Club. The Swiss roll. The mala roast chicken. The durian layer cake. Each one has gone viral on Chinese social media at some point. My mother knows which day the Swiss rolls restock.

Sam's Club has 48 stores in China. Costco has seven.

And the numbers are starting to shift. Sam's Club membership fee revenue grew 14.4% last quarter—first time it's outpaced Costco's 14%. The gap is tiny. But the direction flipped. Their fees are twenty percent cheaper. Their private label, Member's Mark, is being rebuilt from scratch. Over 120 stores have AI-powered checkout.

Still—Kirkland took thirty years to get to $90 billion. Member's Mark has been reinvented for three.

Our team scored them across eight dimensions. Costco: 8.81. Sam's Club: 7.08. The gap is real but smaller than it was five years ago. And closing faster than most people assume.

The most counterintuitive part: Sam's Club expanding might actually help Costco. It normalizes the concept—pay an annual fee, drive to a warehouse, buy in bulk. China didn't have that habit before. Sam's is building the category. When Costco eventually scales up there, half the education is already done.

Sometimes a competitor growing the pie helps everyone at the table.

---

One more thing most Costco shareholders haven't noticed.

Over the past five quarters, not a single Costco insider has bought shares on the open market with their own money.

Not one.

During that stretch, the stock went from $844 to $1,078. Up 27%. Management didn't follow. They accelerated selling. Net insider sales: $5.9 million. Total insider ownership: 0.84%—among the lowest of any large-cap.

You could argue it's routine. Compensation-related. Tax planning. And maybe it is.

But the signal isn't in the selling. Executives sell all the time. The signal is in the **zero buying**.

A stock runs up 27%, and not a single person who knows the business best thought it was worth adding to with their own money. That doesn't mean they think something's wrong. It means they don't think this price is exciting.

Neither do I.

---

Costco trades at 54 times earnings. The consumer staples sector averages 25. Walmart is at 40. Target at 15. Fifty-four is higher than Nvidia.

For that number to hold, you need three things to be true at once.

First, the flywheel is accelerating. We broke it into eight indicators. Four are speeding up: membership fee growth, same-store sales, executive tier upgrades, e-commerce. Four are slowing: renewal rates down four straight quarters, new store plans cut, insiders not buying, Sam's Club catching up. It's not acceleration. It's not collapse. It's a mature company reaching steady state. Steady state supports 45 times. Not 54.

Second, pricing power has no ceiling. Three fee increases—2011, 2017, 2024. Renewal rate never moved more than 0.3 percentage points. Price elasticity near zero. That's real. Costco has arguably the strongest pricing power in global retail. But the next hike—$65 to $75, a 15% jump—would be the largest ever. In a disinflationary environment, the elastic limit hasn't been tested yet.

Third, the brand premium can stretch indefinitely. Our brand assessment model suggests Costco's brand strength and consumer trust support about 41 times earnings. The gap between 41 and 54 is pure narrative—roughly $240 per share in story premium. Stories have value. But story premiums vanish faster than they build.

Three beliefs. All three need to hold. Right now, about one and a half do.

---

Look. Of the thirty-plus companies our team has researched, Costco might have the most beautiful business model of any of them. The membership flywheel. Negative working capital—suppliers effectively float Costco $18.4 billion interest-free. The Kirkland empire. The employee moat. Each one could be its own chapter. Our full report runs 464,000 characters across 25 chapters, and after all that work, our respect for this business only went up.

But 54 times earnings isn't pricing a great company. It's pricing a perfect story.

At checkout I pulled out my membership card. Scanned it. The screen flashed: *Member Since 2019*.

Six years. One move, two job changes. The card stayed in my wallet through all of it.

Ninety-two percent of members do exactly what I do. That's the deepest moat there is.

It's just that the current price tag on that moat—every dollar of earnings times fifty-four—reminds me of something a colleague said after we finished the report:

"Costco is the one you want to marry. But the dowry is insane."
