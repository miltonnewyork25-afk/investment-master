# Row Five

Late February, a Wednesday night. After hours.

Nvidia reported. $68.1 billion in quarterly revenue. Beat expectations by three billion. Full-year revenue up nearly three-quarters. Net income past $120 billion. This is a chip company, not a country.

Best quarter in its history.

Stock dropped almost six percent.

---

A friend in a group chat—quant guy—sent one line: "Numbers are fine. Market's pricing something else."

Pricing what?

I pulled up something that wasn't the earnings report. It was the forecast tables from the same analysts who rated the stock Strong Buy.

Thirty-eight out of forty said buy. Average price target nearly fifty percent above current. But if you scroll their projections two more years out—to FY2030, the fifth row—a quiet number shows up.

Revenue growth turns negative.

Not a crash. Just under three points. From $545 billion down to $530 billion.

But that minus sign means something: even the analysts believe the AI buildout peaks before then.

---

This is the contradiction our team keeps going back to.

The stock trades at roughly thirty-six times earnings. Thirty-six times says the market believes this company keeps growing fast for a long time. But the analysts' own curve—four years climbing, then it bends.

You can't price thirty-six times and draw a line that turns down in year five.

Either the analysts are wrong—growth doesn't slow. Or the price is wrong—thirty-six times for a company about to plateau.

---

Why call it a cycle? Not a hunch. A specific set of numbers.

This year, the big tech companies plan to spend roughly $650 to $700 billion on AI infrastructure. That's more than half their revenue.

The last time humanity spent like this on a single technology was the telecom buildout around 2000. At its peak, capital spending hit about a third of revenue.

Now it's over half.

How that ended isn't something I need to spell out.

---

There's a more direct number.

Total global revenue from AI products—not investment, actual money companies pay to use AI—runs about $100 billion a year. The tech giants are pouring roughly $400 billion into building the infrastructure.

Four dollars in. One dollar out.

That gap closes one of two ways: AI revenue catches up, or capital spending stops first. If it's the latter, Nvidia's revenue curve doesn't just slow down. It turns.

---

Our team doesn't agree on this.

One side argues AI is different from telecom. Laying fiber was a one-time buildout—once it's done, you stop. Training models is open-ended—bigger models need more compute, and the curve may not cliff the way it did twenty-five years ago.

The other side points to a number: at the current pace, Amazon's free cash flow this year could go negative. Not slightly negative. Tens of billions negative. Even the richest companies bleed at this rate. The question is how long.

Both sides make sense. I don't know who's right.

---

But one thing is worth sitting with.

A lot of people see Nvidia's P/E dropping from the sixties or seventies last year to the mid-thirties now and think: cheaper.

I get that instinct. If revenue keeps climbing, thirty-six times isn't expensive.

But if revenue flattens by year five—thirty-six times isn't "cheaper." It's "not cheap enough."

The difference depends on how many rows of the forecast you read.

---

That night, after I closed the laptop, the table stayed in my head.

Four rows of numbers, each one higher than the last. Then row five. A quiet minus sign.

Small. Just under three points.

But every disagreement about this company might come down to that single row.
