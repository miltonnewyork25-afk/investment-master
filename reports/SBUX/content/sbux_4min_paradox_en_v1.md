# The Four-Minute Queue

$6.75.

Oat milk latte, extra shot. That's $140 a month if you go five days a week. $1,700 a year. My electric bill for two months. Three years ago the same drink was under five bucks.

Four cars ahead of me in the drive-thru. I didn't honk. Hands on the wheel, stale french fry smell from yesterday's bag wedged in the seat crack, watching the Civic ahead of me puff white exhaust. I used to not think about this. Swipe and go. Now there's always a beat before I order—a quick mental weigh-in that wasn't there before.

My neighbor Jessica switched her Nespresso from official pods to Costco's Kirkland knock-offs last month. Saves forty cents a pod. Over two hundred a year. She called it "not being stupid." Someone in the school parents' group shared a Starbucks copycat recipes link. Twenty-something moms liked it. The $5 t-shirt section at Target doubled in size. Trader Joe's parking lot is full by ten on Saturdays.

People aren't broke. Spending just got heavier.

These people won't show up in Starbucks' rewards data. They used to come once or twice a week—not loyal enough to track, but enough to matter. Now they're drifting away. Not quitting. Just quietly not coming back.

Two cars left. I stared through the window.

---

Two baristas working the bar.

One on the espresso machine, hands fast, but a small pause between each cup—checking the screen for customizations. Oat milk swap, extra shot, light ice, extra foam. The other handling mobile orders: stickering bags, sorting, sliding them onto the pickup counter.

Their paths crossed. At one point both reached for the same cup at the same time.

Neither said a word. One hand pulled back.

I used to work in tech. Once toured a warehouse sorting line that had the exact same problem—not that people were too slow, but that two workflows were fighting over the same physical space.

This isn't a coffee shop with an operations issue. It's a production line trapped inside a retail store.

---

The new CEO, Brian Niccol, made a promise last year: peak-hour drinks in four minutes or less. His words were "maniacal about four minutes." Bold.

A typical store pushes about forty drinks an hour during rush, each with three or four customizations. That puts capacity utilization at just over half. In any factory, that number gets you fired.

He's done real work—algorithm-based sequencing, menu simplification, automated cold drink equipment. Eighty percent of company-operated stores now hit the four-minute mark.

Sounds like problem solved.

Our team ran a different set of numbers. Once the easy levers are pulled, what's left is the hard one: more people behind the bar during peak hours. One or two extra baristas per store. Four to five thousand dollars a month per location. Across all U.S. company-operated stores, that's roughly a billion dollars a year.

Niccol is also pushing a two-billion-dollar cost-cutting plan.

The right hand saves. The left hand spends. We started calling it the four-minute paradox.

The uncomfortable part: the speed is already back. Profits aren't. The operating margin is still nearly half of where it was six years ago. And barista wages keep climbing—past twenty dollars an hour in some states. This isn't a one-time expense. The floor moved up. Permanently.

---

Niccol pulled off something impressive at Chipotle: order time down from twelve minutes to eight. Cut by a third.

But a burrito is a line moving forward—rice, beans, meat, salsa—add a second line and you nearly double throughput. Coffee is one person standing still—grind, tamp, extract, steam, pour—serial, same machine.

About half of what he learned at Chipotle transfers. The other half he has to figure out from scratch. That half is the expensive one.

To be fair, coffee margins per cup are much higher than burritos, so there's more room to absorb labor costs. How much room exactly, our team hasn't settled that argument yet.

---

Got my coffee.

Took a sip. Same taste as three years ago. Just costs a third more now.

Pulling out, I glanced in the rearview. Seven or eight cars behind me. You won't find "four-minute paradox" as a line item in any financial statement.

But everyone who's ever sat in a drive-thru doing mental math is part of it.
