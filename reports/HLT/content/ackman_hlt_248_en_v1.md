# 248%

Booked a Hampton Inn the week before Thanksgiving last year. $189 a night.

Not a Ritz. Not a Four Seasons. The kind off the highway—two stories, free breakfast, parking lot big enough for pickups. Same hotel, Thanksgiving week, three years ago: $139.

The front desk guy was friendly. Gave me two bottles of water. Room was clean. Nothing wrong with it. Nothing interesting about it either. Same as two years ago. Same as five. Faint disinfectant smell in the hallway, AC humming, curtains open to a view of the parking lot and a Waffle House.

Then I remembered: **this room doesn't belong to Hilton.**

It belongs to some franchisee who took out a loan. He built the building, hired the front desk guy, bought those two bottles of water. Hilton charges him a brand licensing fee, a loyalty system access fee, a booking channel commission. Whether the hotel makes money or not, Hilton takes its cut first.

This isn't a hotel company. It's a toll collector.

---

February 2026. Bill Ackman announced at his annual investor meeting: the Hilton position was sold. All of it. Every share.

Bought in 2018. Held for eight years. Through COVID—2020, the hotel industry nearly shut down. He didn't sell. Post-pandemic bounce. Didn't sell. Stock hit all-time highs in 2023 and 2024. Didn't sell.

Then, with the pipeline at a record high and net room additions at their best in company history, he left.

+248%.

---

Not a panic sell. Not a stop-loss. Net room additions for the year: 97,000. All-time best. Management guided 6–7% growth for next year.

The numbers were all good. Then he left.

Why?

Our team finished our Hilton research report before the news of Ackman's exit came out. Two independent lines of work arrived at similar conclusions. That kind of coincidence makes you pay attention.

---

IHG, Marriott, Hilton. Three giants side by side. Nearly identical business models—asset-light, brand licensing, management fees. Read all three annual reports with the names blacked out and you couldn't tell which was which.

The valuations are miles apart. IHG under 28 times earnings. Marriott at 35. Hilton above 50.

Not margins. Not return on capital—Hilton's is actually the lowest of the three. The only variable that explains the gap: **how many new rooms they open each year.** The industry calls it NUG—Net Unit Growth. IHG under five points. Marriott at five. Hilton nearly seven.

The market isn't pricing a hotel company. It's pricing a speed.

We ran a regression across the big three—valuation against NUG. For every one-point change in NUG, the P/E moves by roughly seven times.

Seven.

If Hilton's growth rate drops from seven to six—just one point—the stock could fall over ten percent. Down to four or five, in line with peers? That's not a ten percent conversation anymore.

Some on the team argued the sample was too small. Adding Hyatt and Wyndham widened the range but didn't change the direction. NUG is the single thread holding up the entire valuation.

---

My friend Kevin booked an Airbnb for Christmas last year. Four bedrooms, two baths, full kitchen. $280 a night. "Cheaper than two Hamptons and way more comfortable," he said.

A Hampton, a Courtyard, a Holiday Inn Express—for someone dragging a suitcase down a highway exit at 10 p.m., honestly, I can't tell the difference either.

That's the real reason Hilton's revenue per room barely grew last year. It's not that nobody's staying in hotels. It's that they can't raise prices.

Prices stall. The market shrugs, looks past it, and keeps pricing the growth story.

---

Hilton is borrowing money to buy back its own stock. Free cash flow covers all the buybacks and then some—except it doesn't. They borrow the rest.

We calculated a buyback efficiency metric: for every dollar spent on repurchases, how much does per-share value go up? Six years ago, nearly 4%. Now, under 1%.

Efficiency down by three-quarters.

As long as the market believes the NUG story, the flywheel keeps spinning. High valuation means cheap capital. Cheap capital funds more buybacks. More buybacks push up earnings per share. Higher EPS justifies the high valuation. Link by link, it holds together.

But the same chain works in reverse. Just as fast.

---

CEO Nassetta has been selling shares on a regular schedule the past two years. Not huge amounts—he still holds over $900 million worth. Likely tied to option exercise windows. Doesn't have to mean anything.

But.

One executive selling at this price range. One legendary investor, eight-year holder, selling everything at the same price range. Two independent lines. Same zone.

Maybe it's a coincidence. Maybe.

---

Could I be wrong?

Yes.

Hilton barely owns any real estate. What's on the balance sheet is brands and management contracts, not land and buildings. Using traditional return-on-capital metrics to anchor the valuation may structurally undervalue this kind of model. That argument has merit. Our team has debated it internally and still hasn't fully agreed.

What if NUG stays at seven or higher for the next three years? Global construction starts are accelerating. Management says 2026 openings will be up over twenty percent. If those numbers come through, today's price might just be "expensive"—not "unreasonable."

I accept all of that. Research isn't about proving yourself right.

---

On the drive back I kept thinking about it.

Eight years. Through COVID. Through rate hikes. Through the best year the company ever had. Then he walked away.

I don't know if he's right. But if one day the NUG numbers start getting smaller, I'll think back to that Thanksgiving—the disinfectant smell, the two bottles of water, and the man who left during the best year.
