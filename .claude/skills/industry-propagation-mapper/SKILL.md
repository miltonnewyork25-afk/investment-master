# Industry Propagation Mapper

## What this skill does
This skill maps how a real trend spreads across an industry and its adjacent layers.

It is used to answer:

- what is the core of the trend?
- what industries or sub-layers are directly pulled first?
- what industries are pulled later?
- which nodes are true economic beneficiaries?
- which nodes are only weak thematic spillovers?
- how does the propagation sequence unfold over time?

The goal is not just to draw a value chain.
The goal is to identify the real transmission path of profit and attention.

---

## Core idea
A trend does not affect every company or layer equally or at the same time.

It usually spreads in waves:

1. Core layer
2. First adjacent layer
3. Second adjacent layer
4. Outer layer
5. Narrative spillover layer

This skill helps distinguish:
- true propagation
from
- superficial association

---

## Default workflow

### Step 1: Define the trend core
Identify:
- the original source of demand
- the immediate product or system bottleneck
- the first economic owner

Ask:
- what absolutely must happen first?
- what cannot be skipped?

### Step 2: Map the direct chain
List the nodes that directly enable the trend.

These usually include:
- key inputs
- core infrastructure
- required manufacturing
- must-have components
- enabling services

### Step 3: Map adjacent layers
Identify second-order and third-order beneficiaries.

Ask:
- what becomes necessary after the core ramps?
- what benefits only after deployment, scaling, or utilization rises?
- what grows because the first layer now needs support?

### Step 4: Classify propagation strength
For each node or layer, classify the linkage:

- Core / indispensable
- First-order / strong transmission
- Second-order / conditional transmission
- Third-order / indirect transmission
- Weak thematic spillover
- False linkage

### Step 5: Map time sequence
Estimate the order in which layers should move economically, not just narratively.

Use categories such as:
- immediate
- early
- mid-cycle
- delayed
- long-tail

Ask:
- who benefits first?
- who only benefits after capex?
- who only benefits after volume?
- who only benefits after installed base expansion?
- who only benefits after replacement / maintenance begins?

### Step 6: Compare economic propagation vs market propagation
Identify where:
- economics are already propagating but market attention is not
- market attention has already propagated but economics are not yet there

This is often where mispricing lives.

---

## What to look for

### Core transmission signals
- capex and budget commitments
- direct supply agreements
- long-term contracts
- order and backlog growth
- qualification to volume transition
- capacity additions
- utilization bottlenecks
- input scarcity
- replacement or maintenance necessity

### Outer propagation signals
- supplier utilization
- second-order order growth
- ecosystem hiring and expansion
- aftermarket demand
- support infrastructure demand
- maintenance, software, testing, cooling, power, logistics, financing, or services pull-through

---

## What good use looks like
A good output should make clear:

- what the true core of the trend is
- what layers are economically central
- what layers are adjacent but real
- what layers are mostly narrative spillover
- what the likely time order is
- where the market is still looking at the wrong layer

---

## Key reminders

### 1. Do not flatten the chain
A value chain is not enough.
You must distinguish:
- core
- adjacent
- outer
- narrative spillover

### 2. Do not confuse mention frequency with economic importance
The most discussed layer is not always the most important one.

### 3. Do not confuse adjacency with transmission
Just because a company is near the trend does not mean economics will transmit meaningfully.

### 4. Propagation is time-dependent
The right expression layer may change over time.

The best layer early may be:
- core enabler

Later it may be:
- constrained supplier

Later still it may be:
- service / aftermarket / maintenance owner

### 5. Outer layers are not automatically worse
Sometimes outer layers have:
- cleaner margins
- less crowding
- stronger expectation gaps
- better residual upside

---

## Suggested thinking structure

When using this skill, think in the following sequence:

1. What is the trend core?
2. What must happen first?
3. What gets pulled next?
4. What only benefits after scale?
5. What only benefits after deployment?
6. What is real transmission?
7. What is weak spillover?
8. What layer has the best current setup?

---

## Flexibility
This skill is a guide, not a rigid map.

Not every trend propagates in the same way.
Some trends stay concentrated in the core.
Some spread into infrastructure and services.
Some create false outer-layer rallies without real economics.

If the real propagation path differs from the default layered model, follow the evidence.

---

## Ideal use cases
Use this skill when:
- a new technology wave is emerging
- a new infrastructure buildout is underway
- a new regulation or standard creates layered adoption
- the core winners are already obvious
- you want to identify the next layer of beneficiaries
- you want to separate real chain transmission from hype spread

---

## Simple example logic
For an AI infrastructure wave:

- Core: accelerators / training clusters / hyperscaler capex
- First adjacent: foundry, advanced packaging, HBM, optical interconnect
- Second adjacent: timing, substrates, materials, thermal management, power distribution
- Third adjacent: maintenance, software optimization, monitoring, field services, financing
- Weak spillover: generic "AI exposure" names with no real transmission path

The skill should help determine:
- what is essential
- what is early adjacent
- what is delayed but real
- what is only thematic

## Extensions to test (pending real-case validation, not default workflow)

Two candidate node types to flag during propagation mapping, for case-level validation before promoting to default workflow:

**Bridge node hypothesis** — a node that sits on ≥2 *independent* L1 forced demand chains (not two expressions of the same demand). During mapping, flag any node that appears in more than one chain diagram.

**Duration node hypothesis** — a node whose economics extend into replacement / maintenance / service layers with monetization window ≥ 2× the initial deployment cycle. During Step 6 time-sequencing, flag long-tail layer owners explicitly.

Validation cases queued: GE Aerospace (duration), Quanta (bridge). Upgrade this skill only after ≥1 case where v1 demonstrably failed to surface these nodes.
