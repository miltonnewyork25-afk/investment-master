# Row Five

I've never owned Nvidia.

Not for lack of thinking about it. I thought about it two years ago when it doubled. Thought about it again a year later when it doubled once more. Each time I told myself it was too expensive. Each time I was wrong. After a while it wasn't that I didn't want to buy — it was that I couldn't afford to think about it. Thinking meant facing the fact that you watched it walk away from you, and now you have to decide whether to chase.

Chase, and you might be buying the top. Don't chase, and it keeps going, and you get another night staring at the ceiling wondering why you didn't just buy.

I'm guessing I'm not the only one.

---

That feeling came back recently. Someone in a group chat dropped a screenshot — an open-source AI agent called OpenClaw, live for a few weeks, already eating more than a tenth of global AI inference traffic. One app. A friend replied: see, the demand is real. Token consumption this year could be multiples of last year.

Every time a headline like that lands, the same voice starts up: you're running out of time.

---

Late February. Wednesday night. After hours.

Nvidia reported. $68.1 billion in quarterly revenue — beat by three billion. Full-year revenue up nearly three-quarters. Net income past $120 billion. This is a semiconductor company, not a country's GDP.

Best quarter in its history.

Stock dropped almost six percent.

---

A quant friend sent one line: "Numbers are fine. Market's pricing something else."

Pricing what?

I went looking — not at the earnings report itself, but at the forecast tables built by the same analysts who stamped it Strong Buy.

Thirty-eight out of forty said buy. Average target nearly half above current. But if you scroll their projections two more years out — to FY2030, the fifth row — a quiet number appears.

Revenue growth turns negative.

Not a crash. Just under three points. $545 billion down to $530 billion.

But that minus sign says something: even the analysts think the AI buildout peaks before then.

---

This is the contradiction our team keeps circling.

The stock sits at roughly thirty-six times earnings. Thirty-six times means the market believes this company keeps growing fast for a long time. But the analysts' own curve — four years climbing, fifth year bending down.

You can't pay thirty-six times and draw a line that turns in year five.

Either the analysts are wrong — growth doesn't slow. Or the price is wrong — thirty-six times for a company approaching a plateau.

---

Why think of this as a cycle? Not a gut feeling. A specific set of numbers.

This year, big tech plans to spend roughly $650 to $700 billion on AI infrastructure. That's north of half their revenue.

The last time humanity threw money at a single technology like this was the telecom buildout around 2000. At its peak, capital spending hit about a third of revenue.

Now it's past half.

I don't need to tell you how that one ended.

---

There's a more direct number.

Total global revenue from AI products — not investment, actual money companies pay to use AI — runs about $100 billion a year. The tech giants are putting roughly $400 billion into building the pipes.

Four dollars in. One dollar out.

Then last month, OpenAI cut its own multi-year spending plan by $800 billion. The most aggressive AI company on earth looked at its budget and pulled back.

That gap closes one of two ways: AI revenue catches up, or capital spending stops first. Someone already blinked.

---

Our team doesn't agree on this one.

The bulls point to the lobster. AI shifted from chatbots to agents, and inference compute went from a third of the workload to two-thirds in two years. The demand curve isn't peaking. It's shifting gears.

The bears point to a different set of numbers. One study found ninety percent of companies that adopted AI saw zero productivity impact. Money spent, nothing gained. And the five biggest tech spenders are now burning through ninety percent of their cash flow on capex — the ten-year average is forty. The gap is being plugged with debt.

The lobster is real. The debt is real too.

Both sides make sense. I don't know who's right.

---

But for someone like me — someone who's been watching from the sidelines the whole time — all of these arguments eventually collapse into a single question: can I buy it now?

A lot of people see the P/E falling from the sixties or seventies last year to the mid-thirties and think: finally. Cheaper. A reasonable price at last. The voice kicks in again.

I know that voice. I've heard it myself.

But if Nvidia's revenue starts flattening by year five — thirty-six times isn't "cheaper." It's "not cheap enough." That price that finally looks reasonable might just be a different kind of expensive.

The difference depends on how far down the forecast you read.

---

That night, after I closed the laptop, the question was still there.

Not because the answer changed. Because I finally understood what I was really asking. Not "is Nvidia a good company" — of course it is. $120 billion in profit doesn't leave much to argue about. The question was simpler and harder: a great company, at this price, is it a great investment?

I opened the forecast table one more time. Four rows of numbers, each higher than the last. Then row five. A quiet minus sign.

Small. Just under three points.

But my answer might be sitting right there.
