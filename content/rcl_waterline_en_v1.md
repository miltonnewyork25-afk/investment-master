# The Waterline

First day on a cruise ship, you notice something small.

Every crew member wears a name tag. Name and country. Jonathan, Philippines. Sari, Indonesia. Dmitri, Romania. It looks like a celebration of diversity. It isn't. It's a sorting label. The country on your chest decides which deck you sleep on.

---

Every ship has a waterline. Above it is everything you paid for. Balcony suites, infinity pools, lobster buffets. Below it is everything you don't see.

The crew corridor that runs bow to stern on Deck 0 has a nickname: I-95. Same as the highway up the East Coast. Blue carpet, industrial lighting, duty rosters taped to the walls. At two in the morning, while you're asleep in your stateroom, someone walks out of I-95 pushing a vacuum cleaner to go scrub the deck you walked on.

Crew quarters sit below the waterline. Two to four people in a cabin smaller than a parking space. Bunk beds, shared bathroom. Near the stern you feel the engines through the mattress all night. Near the bow you hear the anchor chain drop. Earplugs are not optional. No sunlight. Contracts run six to ten months. No days off. Ten to fourteen hours a day, seven days a week.

A waiter named Jonathan, from Bohol in the Philippines, spent ten years at sea. Missed the birth of all four of his children. Every time, he was somewhere mid-Atlantic, serving dessert to someone's table. His two oldest put themselves through college on that money.

A head sommelier named Alejandro, from Cavite, has been doing this for twenty-seven years. Three kids. Two graduated. One to go. Twenty-seven years of contracts means roughly half his life spent away from home.

A hundred people apply for every opening. Retention runs above eighty percent. Not because life on board is good. Because the math works better than anything back home.

---

That math depends on a structure.

Royal Caribbean's parent company is incorporated in Liberia. West Africa. Its ships fly the Bahamian flag. One entity in Africa, the other in the Caribbean. Neither in the United States.

This structure makes two things disappear at once: U.S. corporate tax and U.S. labor law.

On the tax side, it works through Section 883 of the U.S. tax code — a carve-out for international shipping income. Last year, the company earned close to $3 billion in profit and paid $46 million in tax. For every hundred dollars earned, a dollar sixty went to the government. An American company at the same profit level would owe five or six hundred million.

On the labor side, the Bahamian flag does the work. No minimum wage, no cap on weekly hours, no mandatory time off. There's a line that gets passed around ships — "If you don't like it, you can go home. Ticket's on you."

One structure. Two things gone. The money owed to the government, and the protection owed to the workers.

---

In 2020, the bill came due.

U.S. airlines got over $54 billion in government relief — loans, grants, payroll protection, the full package. Cruise lines got zero. Congress didn't have to think hard about it: you're incorporated in Liberia. You're not an American company.

The structure that saved the tax locked out the safety net.

For fifteen months, Royal Caribbean's revenue was zero. But a hundred thousand crew members couldn't just be let go — maritime labor conventions require repatriation, contracts are binding, and you can't walk off a ship in the middle of an ocean. Cash burn ran close to $300 million a month. Seventy thousand people stuck in steel hulls, going nowhere, earning nothing, while the company bled.

Five or six hundred million a year in tax savings. Three hundred million a month in shutdown costs. The trade works — as long as it doesn't happen often.

Our team ran the numbers. Academic estimates put a COVID-scale zero-revenue event at roughly once every thirty-five years. Yet the market gives Royal Caribbean a P/E of twenty times — more than double Delta's nine. The company with longer shutdowns, faster cash burn, and no government rescue trades at a higher multiple than the one with all three advantages.

I don't have an explanation for that.

---

Now look at the margin.

Last year's operating margin: 27.4 percent. Up more than eight points from five years ago. Wall Street calls it a structural renaissance.

Our team took the number apart. The share of yield growth that's genuinely structural — brand upgrades, permanent shifts in onboard spending — comes to roughly a third. The rest is inflation pass-through, post-COVID supply scarcity, and cycle tailwinds.

There's also a hidden kicker. Interest expense dropped $600 million last year — from $1.6 billion to $1 billion — after a credit upgrade from junk to investment grade. That single line item accounts for more than forty percent of last year's earnings-per-share growth.

That's not operational improvement. That's a one-time refinancing windfall. But the market applies twenty times earnings to the whole number, as if it recurs every year.

---

One more thing that's hard to square.

The company spent $1.16 billion buying back its own stock last year. Management wants to push annual shareholder returns to $2 billion. But free cash flow was only $1.2 billion. The $800 million gap has to come from new debt.

Borrowing to buy back. It's a classic late-cycle move — management knows, or suspects, that the high-margin window won't last, so they rush to return capital while the numbers still look good.

But here's the part that sticks. CEO Jason Liberty has been a net seller of his own shares for eight straight quarters. The company's money is buying. His money is selling.

Talking bull. Walking bear.

I don't know which signal to trust. But SEC Form 4 filings are hard to fake.

---

Pull up the cost structure and scroll to the crew line.

A hundred and six thousand people. Roughly a fifth of all revenue — the single largest cost item. And it's quasi-fixed. Crew are on contracts. Whether the ship sails at full occupancy or half, you pay.

That's why the operating leverage is so violent in both directions. Yield goes up one point, EBITDA goes up three and a half to four. Feels great on the way up.

In 2008, revenue fell less than ten percent. Net income fell seventy-seven percent.

Because the hundred thousand people below the waterline still get paid. Every dollar.

---

What does the current stock price require?

We ran it backwards. To justify today's valuation, you need EBITDA growing twelve percent a year for ten straight years. Global cruise penetration tripling from under three percent to nearly nine. Yield growth holding at seven percent — against next year's guidance of one to one and a half, barely keeping up with inflation. And no zero-revenue event for a full decade.

The odds of all five conditions holding at once come to roughly one in six.

The market is paying full price for a one-in-six outcome.

---

The ships are impressive. Icon of the Seas pulls in over $3 million a day. CocoCay's return on investment is north of two hundred percent. Management builds a hell of a product.

But a good product and a good price are different questions.

Every time I come back to this company, I end up at the same line.

Above the waterline: record margins, sold-out sailings, a stock that won't quit. Below it: a hundred thousand people on fixed contracts, an offshore structure cutting both ways, and a valuation that needs six things to go right at once.

Same ship. Same ledger.
